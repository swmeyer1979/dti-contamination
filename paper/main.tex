\begin{abstract}
On a novel-chemistry holdout of 1{,}028 drug-target pairs gated at Tanimoto $<$ 0.4
versus ChEMBL27, a randomly-initialised ESM-2 + ChemBERTa transformer achieves
higher macro-averaged per-target Pearson $r$ than its pretrained counterpart
($\Delta = +0.14$; BCa 95\% CI $[+0.006, +0.278]$, percentile Fisher-$z$
CI $[-0.012, +0.283]$;
$\Pr[\Delta > 0 \mid \text{bootstrap}] = 0.978$).
This paradoxical result is the signature of three evaluation artifacts that
compound on drug-target interaction (DTI) benchmarks.
First, a single kinase (Q92918/MAP3K1) contributes 53\% of the pairs in a
standard ChEMBL-derived holdout; pooled Pearson $r$ is dominated by this one
target's statistics.
Second, pair-level bootstrap---the literature convention for reporting CIs on DTI
benchmarks---undercovers by 5$\times$: on a synthetic clustered data-generating
process, 95\% pair-bootstrap CIs achieve 17\% coverage, while two-way
protein-compound cluster bootstrap achieves 76\%.
Third, under macro-averaged per-target $r$ with a minimum-$n$ cutoff, none of
four baselines (ESM-2 + ChemBERTa probe, DeepDTA, fixed-projection random probe,
random-initialised transformer) is individually distinguishable from zero on
novel chemistry; the random-init transformer wins only because the pretrained
control loses.
We argue that macro-averaged per-target $r$ with protein-cluster bootstrap CIs
is the minimum-defensible primary metric for DTI benchmarks.
These results do not establish that random initialization is a superior
strategy; they function as a diagnostic that the evaluation pipeline is
unstable enough for a random-init control to edge out a pretrained model
under target-balanced aggregation on this holdout.
The finding replicates on TDC DTI-DG (BindingDB\_Patent, 190 qualifying
targets): random-init macro-avg $r = +0.078$ vs.\ ESM-2 $+0.029$.
Code, data, and analyses: \href{https://github.com/swmeyer1979/dti-contamination}{\texttt{github.com/swmeyer1979/dti-contamination}}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Drug-target interaction (DTI) models are typically evaluated on two benchmarks:
Davis \citep{davis2011comprehensive} (72 inhibitors profiled against 442 kinases) and
KIBA \citep{tang2014making} (the widely-used 2{,}111-compound $\times$ 229-target
ML benchmark filtered from Tang et al.'s original 52{,}498-compound $\times$
467-kinase activity matrix).
Published Pearson $r$ values on these benchmarks routinely exceed $0.85$
\citep{ozturk2018deepdta,nguyen2021graphdta,huang2021moltrans,ross2022large}, a
number that reads as "the model mostly solves DTI for these targets."
Evaluations on structurally novel chemistry---compounds outside the training
manifold---are rarer, but when reported they tend to collapse, with Pearson $r$
dropping by $0.5$ or more \citep{wallach2018most,bender2022evaluation}.

This paper examines three evaluation artifacts that affect both the
in-distribution and novel-chemistry cases.
We construct a clean holdout of 1{,}028 post-2021 ChEMBL34 pairs gated at
Tanimoto $<$ 0.4 and evaluate four baselines on it: a frozen ESM-2 + ChemBERTa
probe, DeepDTA from scratch, a fixed-projection random probe, and a
randomly-initialised ESM-2 + ChemBERTa transformer of identical architecture to
the pretrained probe but without pretrained weights.
The pretrained probe achieves pooled Pearson $r = 0.034$ on the holdout; the
randomly-initialised transformer achieves $-0.042$.
Under a standard reading of these numbers, the pretrained model is slightly
better, and both are essentially zero.

Inspecting the test set reveals that one protein---Q92918/MAP3K1---contributes
428 of 1{,}028 pairs (42\%) and 428 of 801 in the pre-expansion kinase-only
holdout (53\%). Under macro-averaged per-target Pearson $r$ (equal weight per
target, minimum 10 pairs per target), the ranking inverts.
The randomly-initialised transformer achieves macro-avg $r = +0.103$ across 12
qualifying targets; the pretrained probe achieves $-0.035$.
A pairwise target-level bootstrap gives BCa 95\% CI $[+0.006, +0.278]$;
under Fisher-$z$ transformation the CI is $[-0.012, +0.283]$, which includes
zero (see Section~\ref{sec:random-init}).
$\Pr[\Delta > 0 \mid \text{bootstrap}] = 0.978$ (one-sided bootstrap superiority
probability, not a frequentist $p$-value).
The result is preserved across minimum-$n$ cutoffs in $\{5, 10, 20, 30\}$ and
under leave-one-target-out.

This is not plausibly a claim that random initialization is better than
pretraining for DTI.
It is a claim that \emph{the evaluation is broken in a specific, diagnosable way},
and that the pretrained model's apparent advantage under pooled $r$ is an
artifact of single-target dominance compounded by CIs that assume pair
independence that the data structure does not support.
The random-init-wins phenomenon mirrors findings in genomics
\citep{vishniakov2024genomic} and other specialised domains
\citep{xu2025specialized}; the contribution here is the causal decomposition
specific to DTI: metric choice and target concentration, not representation
quality, explain the effect.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Single-target dominance:} one protein (Q92918) contributes 53\%
    of pairs in a standard kinase-only ChEMBL-derived holdout and 42\% of the
    expanded 1{,}028-pair unified holdout. Pooled Pearson $r$ is dominated by
    this one target's statistics in both.
  \item \textbf{Pair-bootstrap CIs undercover by $\sim$5$\times$:} a synthetic
    clustered data-generating process with known true Pearson $r$ shows
    empirical 95\% CI coverage of 17\% for pair-level bootstrap versus 76\% for
    two-way protein-compound cluster bootstrap.
  \item \textbf{Random-initialized transformer paradox:} under macro-averaged
    per-target Pearson $r$, a randomly-initialised ESM-2/ChemBERTa transformer
    (identical architecture, no pretrained weights) outperforms its pretrained
    counterpart on novel chemistry. We argue this is an artifact-diagnostic, not
    a claim that random initialization is a better pretraining strategy.
\end{enumerate}

We do not release a new benchmark and do not claim to solve DTI evaluation.
We claim that the standard pooled-$r$-plus-pair-bootstrap pipeline
misreports model performance on Davis-family benchmarks under DeepDTA-style
cold-drug splits, in the direction of \emph{over-confident} claims about
pretrained-model competence, and that two simple fixes---macro-averaged
per-target $r$ with a minimum-$n$ cutoff, and two-way cluster bootstrap
CIs---recover qualitatively different conclusions on the same data.
The KIBA in-distribution test set (balanced, 229 targets) does not exhibit
the same concentration pathology; the artifacts we describe are specific to
novel-chemistry evaluation with long-tailed target distributions.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{DTI benchmarks.}
Davis \citep{davis2011comprehensive} and KIBA \citep{tang2014making} have been
the canonical DTI benchmarks since DeepDTA \citep{ozturk2018deepdta}
standardised their train/test splits.
Both datasets consist almost entirely of human kinases, with dense coverage in
pretraining corpora.
\citet{pahikkala2015toward} introduced four cross-validation settings
(pair/new-drug/new-target/new-both) for Davis and KIBA, demonstrating that
pair-level random splits are optimistic because target identity dominates
predictive signal.
This structural uniformity has been critiqued as a generalisation limitation
\citep{wallach2018most,bender2022evaluation}, but the consequence for
\emph{pointwise-metric reporting on a given holdout}---that one target can
arithmetically dominate the aggregate score---has not, to our knowledge,
been quantified explicitly.

\paragraph{Pretrained models vs.\ random initialisation.}
The failure of pretrained representations to beat task-specific baselines
has been documented across domains.
\citet{vishniakov2024genomic} show that randomly-initialised models match or
outperform pretrained genomic foundation models on Nucleotide Transformer
Benchmark, GUE, and Genomics Benchmark.
\citet{xu2025specialized} extend this finding across genomics, satellite
imaging, and time-series tasks, arguing that specialised foundation models
struggle to beat supervised baselines in their target domains.
Our random-init result in DTI is a specific instance of this pattern;
the contribution is the causal diagnosis: under target-balanced aggregation,
the apparent pretrained advantage is attributable to
single-target dominance rather than genuine representational superiority.

\paragraph{Evaluation artifacts in ML benchmarks.}
Benchmark concentration and dependence structure have received attention in
adjacent fields.
\citet{wallach2018most} show that ligand-based classification benchmarks reward
memorisation rather than generalisation.
\citet{chen2019hidden} document hidden biases in DUD-E that inflate structure-based
virtual screening performance.
\citet{bouthillier2021accounting} argue that ML benchmark variance is
systematically underestimated because multiple sources of dependence are ignored.
In NLP, \citet{sogaard2014test} and subsequent work show that naive pair-level
test statistics overstate significance when tokens/sentences within a document
are correlated.
In information retrieval and recommendation, macro-averaged per-query metrics
have been preferred over pooled metrics for decades specifically because
per-query relevance counts are long-tailed \citep{voorhees2004overview}.

\paragraph{Cluster-robust inference.}
Cluster-robust variance estimation is standard in econometrics and biostatistics
\citep{cameron2015practitioner,liang1986longitudinal}.
The two-way (multi-way) cluster bootstrap generalises pair-level resampling to
account for two independent clustering dimensions
\citep{cameron2011robust,efron1979bootstrap}.
For situations with few clusters, wild cluster bootstrap
\citep{mackinnon2017wild} improves coverage; recent jackknife alternatives
(CV3/CV3J) are more robust to heterogeneous cluster sizes
\citep{mackinnon2023fast}.
Protein interaction benchmarks have recently been audited for similar leakage
patterns \citep{bushuiev2024revealing}.
This paper's contribution is applying cluster-robust tooling to DTI benchmarks
specifically and documenting that it changes the empirical conclusions;
we use the CGM two-way cluster bootstrap \citep{cameron2011robust} and
acknowledge that CV3J or wild bootstrap would improve coverage further
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Pretrained models for DTI.}
Frozen pretrained encoders (ESM-2 \citep{lin2023evolutionary}, ChemBERTa
\citep{chithrananda2020chemberta}, MoLFormer \citep{ross2022large}) match or
exceed from-scratch models on Davis and KIBA under pooled $r$.
Whether these gains reflect generalisable representations or benchmark-specific
fit has been debated but not decisively settled.
Our random-init transformer control directly isolates the pretrained-weights
contribution from the transformer architecture's inductive bias.

\section{Data and Methods}

\subsection{Benchmark arms}

\paragraph{Training data.}
We use the canonical train/test splits from \citet{ozturk2018deepdta} for Davis
(27{,}621 pairs after SMILES validation) and KIBA (25{,}045 pairs).
Davis $K_d$ values are converted from raw nM to $\text{p}K_d = -\log_{10}(K_d \cdot
10^{-9})$ before z-scoring so that higher values denote stronger binding,
matching KIBA scores and holdout pChEMBL values.
Per-dataset z-scoring is applied before joint training; Pearson $r$ is
invariant to this rescaling.

\paragraph{Holdout arm.}
The novel-chemistry holdout is constructed from ChEMBL34
\citep{zdrazil2024chembl34} documents deposited in 2022 or later (post-cutoff
for both ESM-2 and ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1 pretraining corpora).
We retain pairs satisfying all of:
(i) $\text{pChEMBL} \geq 5.0$;
(ii) $\text{standard\_type} \in \{K_d, K_i\}$ (binding assays only; IC$_{50}$ is
used only to count per-target activity volume when selecting the protein set,
not as training labels);
(iii) $\text{max Tanimoto} < 0.4$ to any ChEMBL27 compound (Morgan
radius-2, 2048-bit fingerprints via FPSim2).
Where multiple activity records exist for the same (canonical SMILES,
UniProt) pair, we retain the single record with the highest pChEMBL value.
The protein set is the union of (a) Davis/KIBA target UniProt IDs and (b) all
single-protein human targets in ChEMBL34 with $\geq 50$ qualifying post-2022
$K_d$/$K_i$/$\text{IC}_{50}$ activities.
After SMILES validation and deduplication, this yields
\textbf{1{,}028 pairs, 85 unique targets, 941 unique compounds}.

\paragraph{Contamination scope.}
ESM-2 (\path{esm2_t33_650M_UR50D}) was trained on UniRef50/D with a
2021-04 dataset stamp; the post-2022 document filter is conservative with
respect to its protein sequences.
ChemBERTa (\path{seyonec/ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1}) was trained on
approximately 100k SMILES from ZINC; the exact held-out split is not publicly
released, so we use Tanimoto $< 0.4$ versus ChEMBL27 as a structural-novelty
proxy.
This proxy is conservative---it gates on training-corpus vintage rather than
confirmed exclusion---so the contamination status of individual compounds
relative to the ChemBERTa training corpus is approximate.
Results should be interpreted accordingly; we make no strong claim that every
compound in the holdout was unseen by the small-molecule encoder.

\paragraph{Q92918 concentration.}
One UniProt accession, Q92918 (MAP3K1, a serine/threonine kinase), contributes
428 of the 1{,}028 holdout pairs (41.6\%).
In the pre-expansion kinase-only holdout (801 pairs), Q92918 contributed 428
(53.4\%).
Q92918 is the dominant target in both constructions.

\subsection{Models}

\paragraph{ESM-2 + ChemBERTa probe (pretrained).}
Frozen pretrained ESM-2 (\path{facebook/esm2_t33_650M_UR50D}) and ChemBERTa
(\path{seyonec/ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1}) encoders.
CLS embeddings (dimensions 1{,}280 and 768) are concatenated and passed to a
three-layer MLP ($2048 \to 512 \to 256 \to 1$, ReLU, 10\% dropout, Adam at
$10^{-3}$, batch 512, 50 epochs).

\paragraph{DeepDTA (from scratch).}
Character-level 1D CNNs on amino-acid and SMILES strings, end-to-end for 100
epochs, following \citet{ozturk2018deepdta}.

\paragraph{Random-projection probe.}
Identical MLP and training procedure to the ESM-2 probe, but protein and compound
representations are fixed unit-normalised random Gaussian vectors (seeds 0 and 1)
of dimensions 1{,}280 and 768.

\paragraph{Random-initialized transformer.}
Identical architecture to the ESM-2 + ChemBERTa probe (same \texttt{EsmConfig} and
\texttt{RobertaConfig} loaded via HuggingFace), but with randomly-initialised
weights instead of pretrained weights.
CLS embeddings are row-normalised before concatenation (otherwise random weights
produce unstable magnitudes).
Same MLP head and training procedure as the pretrained probe.

This ablation isolates the contribution of pretrained weights from the
transformer's architectural inductive bias (attention, positional encoding).
Both pretrained and random-init variants use the same MLP head, learning rate
($10^{-3}$), batch size, and training duration.
We acknowledge that pretrained models may benefit from a lower fine-tuning
learning rate; however, both variants here train the full model
(encoders $+$ MLP head) from the same starting procedure, so the comparison
is hyperparameter-symmetric in that sense.
A lower-LR fine-tuning regime for the pretrained variant remains a useful
follow-up ablation.

\subsection{Metrics and inference}
\label{sec:methods-inference}

\paragraph{Pooled Pearson $r$.}
$r_\text{pool} = \text{Pearson}(y_\text{true}, y_\text{pred})$ across all test
pairs. This is the literature convention.

\paragraph{Macro-averaged per-target Pearson $r$.}
For each target $t$ with at least $n_\text{min}$ pairs and non-zero variance in
both $y_\text{true}$ and $y_\text{pred}$, compute $r_t$. Then
\[
  r_\text{macro} = \frac{1}{|T|} \sum_{t \in T} r_t
\]
where $T$ is the set of qualifying targets. Primary analysis uses
$n_\text{min} = 10$; sensitivity to this cutoff is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}.

\paragraph{Pair-level bootstrap CI.}
Resample test pairs with replacement, 10{,}000 iterations. Percentile 95\% CI.
This is the literature convention \citep{ozturk2018deepdta}.

\paragraph{Two-way (protein $\times$ compound) cluster bootstrap CI.}
For each iteration, draw protein cluster counts $k_p \sim
\text{Multinomial}(K_p, 1/K_p)$ over the $K_p$ unique proteins and compound
cluster counts $k_c \sim \text{Multinomial}(K_c, 1/K_c)$ over the $K_c$
unique compounds, independently.
Each pair $i = (p, c)$ receives weight $w_i = k_p[p] \cdot k_c[c]$.
Compute weighted Pearson $r$ on the resulting sample.
10{,}000 iterations, percentile 95\% CI.

\paragraph{Target-level bootstrap.}
For macro-averaged per-target $r$: resample targets (not pairs) with
replacement and recompute $r_\text{macro}$ per iteration. This is the correct
inference procedure for comparing two models under the macro-averaged metric.
Pairwise model comparisons use paired target-level resampling on the common
target set.

\subsection{Pair-level bootstrap undercovers by $\sim$5$\times$}
\label{sec:coverage}

We quantify the extent to which pair-level bootstrap CIs miss their nominal
coverage on data with cluster structure resembling DTI benchmarks.

A synthetic data-generating process is specified: 80 proteins $\times$ 100 pairs
per protein, with 300 unique compounds shared across proteins.
True affinity $= \text{protein\_effect} + \text{compound\_effect} +
\text{noise}$, where protein and compound effects are drawn from normal
distributions with standard deviations 1.0 and 0.6.
A linear model prediction equals $0.8 \cdot \text{true\_affinity} + \text{noise}$.
The reference (large-sample) Pearson $r$ of the DGP is $0.915$.

We run 300 replications at 500 bootstrap iterations each and measure the
empirical coverage rate of 95\% CIs.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Empirical 95\% CI coverage on a synthetic two-way clustered DGP
(300 replications, true $r = 0.915$).}
\label{tab:coverage}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Bootstrap} & \textbf{Empirical coverage} & \textbf{Mean CI width} \\
\midrule
Pair-level                 & 0.170 & 0.006 \\
Compound cluster (one-way) & 0.210 & 0.008 \\
Protein cluster (one-way)  & 0.733 & 0.027 \\
\textbf{Two-way cluster}   & \textbf{0.757} & \textbf{0.029} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Pair-level bootstrap achieves 17\% coverage of a nominal 95\% CI.
Compound-cluster bootstrap is only marginally better at 21\%; compound-level
cluster structure is weaker on this DGP because there are more unique compounds
than proteins.
Protein-cluster bootstrap recovers most of the missing coverage at 73\%.
Two-way cluster bootstrap (resampling both dimensions) achieves 76\% and remains
below the nominal 95\%---an honest finding: cluster bootstrap is a substantial
improvement over pair-level but does not fully solve the coverage problem under
strong cluster structure.

For the remainder of this paper we report two-way cluster bootstrap as the
primary inference procedure and pair-level as a literature-comparison footnote.

\begin{enumerate}

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The random-init transformer paradox}
\label{sec:random-init}

The random-init transformer's macro-avg $r = +0.103$ is not significant as a
standalone model-level claim ($95\%$ CI $[-0.102, +0.281]$), but it is
significantly higher than the pretrained ESM-2 probe's macro-avg $r = -0.035$
under a paired target-bootstrap.
Table~\ref{tab:pairwise-delta} reports the pairwise contrasts.

this is a fragile result: the formal superiority claim rests on a lower bound
that is numerically indistinguishable from zero under any of these procedures.
We retain the contrast as a \emph{diagnostic}---the rank reversal is robust
to target weighting, seed variation, and leave-one-out---but do not assert
formal frequentist superiority.
$\Pr[\Delta > 0 \mid \text{bootstrap}] = 0.978$ under percentile and $0.963$
under Fisher-$z$, reflecting strong directional consistency even without
formal significance.

The paradox: a transformer with randomly-initialised weights predicts novel
chemistry better than the same transformer with pretrained weights, when
performance is measured equally across targets.
We discuss three plausible explanations in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{DiD analysis of contamination is unidentifiable}

For completeness we also report a difference-in-differences estimator for the
original pretraining-contamination question: does ESM-2's performance drop
from benchmark to holdout differ from controls' drops in a direction consistent
with contamination?

which control is chosen (Table~\ref{tab:did}).

depending on which metric (pooled vs.\ macro-avg) and which control are
chosen.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why does a randomly-initialized transformer win?}

Three plausible mechanisms. They are not mutually exclusive.

\paragraph{(1) The pretrained probe is actively worse on non-kinase targets.}
Pretrained ESM-2 representations were trained on UniRef50, which is
protein-family-imbalanced.
ESM-2 embeddings of non-kinase targets may land in regions of representation
space that the MLP head, trained predominantly on kinase data, handles poorly.
A randomly-initialised transformer has no such family-specific bias; its
embeddings are target-identifier-like without encoding learned family structure
that the MLP was not trained to use.

\paragraph{(2) Pretrained embeddings encourage family-specific overfitting.}
Pretrained ESM-2 embeddings cluster proteins by evolutionary similarity---kinases
land near kinases. The MLP head, trained almost entirely on kinase data, learns
patterns that exploit this clustering. Those patterns fail on novel-chemistry
pairs where the binding is driven by compound structure rather than by the
already-seen kinase. A randomly-initialised transformer produces embeddings
with no such learned clustering; each target is an arbitrary point in
representation space. The MLP has fewer hooks on which to overfit the kinase
structure, and correspondingly weaker benchmark performance---but also
correspondingly weaker failure on targets outside the training family.

\paragraph{(3) Apparent advantage is small-sample noise within significance.}
The pairwise $\Delta = +0.138$ has 95\% CI with lower bound $+0.004$.
This is barely significant; at 20 qualifying targets instead of 12 the
significance might dissolve.
The finding is real at the sample size we have, but should not be over-read.

None of these mechanisms individually establishes that pretraining is
counter-productive for DTI generalisation.
Collectively they establish that the apparent \emph{advantage} of pretraining
under pooled $r$ on Davis/KIBA is artifactual.
A defensible stronger reading requires more datasets, more pretrained
checkpoints, and a controlled ablation of pretraining corpus composition.

\subsection{What does a DTI benchmark actually measure?}

Under cluster-robust inference and macro-averaged metrics, the central finding
of this paper is negative: \emph{none} of the four baselines tested generalises
meaningfully to novel chemistry on a realistic post-2021 ChEMBL holdout.
The best non-trivially positive number is the random-init transformer at
macro-avg $r = +0.103$, with 95\% CI containing zero.

Published Pearson $r$ values above $0.85$ on Davis and KIBA measure predictive
ability on a specific, concentrated distribution of kinases and compounds.
They do not measure the ability to predict binding affinity for chemistry
outside that distribution.
This is not a new observation \citep{wallach2018most,bender2022evaluation}, but
the Q92918 case shows that even the pooled holdout numbers---which are
supposed to quantify exactly this novel-chemistry behaviour---are
substantially driven by single-target effects rather than broad generalisation.

\subsection{Actionable recommendations for DTI evaluation}

We recommend three concrete reporting changes.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Macro-averaged per-target $r$ as primary metric}, with
    $n_\text{min} \geq 10$ pairs per target.
    Pooled $r$ should be reported only as a secondary metric, clearly flagged
    as sensitive to pair-count imbalance.
  \item \textbf{Per-target pair-count distribution reported in every paper.}
    A single line---``top-5 targets account for $X\%$ of test pairs''---would
    make the concentration visible to reviewers and readers.
  \item \textbf{Two-way (protein $\times$ compound) cluster bootstrap CIs}, or
    a principled alternative (mixed-effects, cluster-robust sandwich
    estimator).
    Pair-level CIs should not be reported as the primary inference procedure on
    DTI benchmarks.
\end{enumerate}

These changes are additive to existing practice; they do not require
re-running any baselines or redesigning any benchmarks.

\section{Limitations}

\paragraph{Small number of qualifying targets and bootstrap coverage.}
At $n_\text{min} = 10$ the holdout yields only 12 qualifying targets.
$K = 12$ is well inside the ``danger zone'' ($K < 30$) identified by
\citet{cameron2015practitioner} for target-level bootstrap inference.
The raw-percentile bootstrap on Pearson $r$ without Fisher-$z$ transformation
is known to under-cover at small $K$ \citep{mackinnon2023fast}; under a
realistic 85--88\% effective coverage, the reported CIs are fragile: the
Fisher-$z$ CI $[-0.012, +0.283]$ already includes zero, and the BCa lower
bound ($+0.006$) is numerically near-zero.
Fisher-$z$ transformation with BCa or studentised bootstrap-$t$ intervals
\citep{mackinnon2023fast} would provide more reliable coverage;
we report the percentile bootstrap as the primary procedure and note this
as a limitation.
The diagnostic value of the rank reversal (8 of 12 targets, distributed
across protein families) does not depend on formal significance, but formal
superiority claims should be treated as indicative rather than conclusive.

\paragraph{External replication and multiplicity.}
The concentration artifact and random-init-wins finding replicate on TDC
DTI-DG (BindingDB\_Patent, 190 qualifying targets, Section~\ref{sec:tdc}).
The full rank ordering does not replicate exactly across both datasets.
Table~\ref{tab:pairwise-delta} and Table~\ref{tab:cutoff-sensitivity} scan
multiple contrasts and cutoffs without multiplicity correction; a strict
Bonferroni adjustment would widen the effective threshold.
The diagnostic rank reversal is robust to cutoff choice and leave-one-out
perturbation, but the formal pairwise superiority claim rests on a single
contrast that barely clears a standard threshold.
A companion analysis on other temporal holdouts (BindingDB, TDC DTI-DG, or
ChEMBL-based splits used by newer foundation models) would test whether
the concentration pattern and rank reversal replicate across data sources;
we expect they would, since the underlying cause---long-tailed target
distribution in post-cutoff bioactivity data---is not specific to
our construction.

\paragraph{Single pretrained model.}
We evaluate one pretrained configuration: ESM-2 \path{(t33_650M_UR50D)} with
ChemBERTa \path{(zinc-base-v1)}.
Stronger claims about pretraining broadly would require additional foundation
models (MoLFormer, ChemBERTa-3, ESM-3).

\paragraph{Multi-seed random initialisation.}
We ran the random-init transformer with three seeds $\{7, 42, 99\}$.
Per-seed macro-avg $r$ values are $+0.103$, $+0.172$, and $+0.078$;
multi-seed mean is $+0.138$.
All three seeds outperform the pretrained ESM-2 probe ($-0.035$), and the
pooled-prediction macro-avg ($+0.138$) exceeds the single-seed primary result.
The variance across seeds ($0.078$ to $0.172$) is itself evidence that
the rank advantage is modest rather than structural.
Multi-seed predictions are in
\path{results/random_transformer_multiseed_predictions.parquet}.

\paragraph{Cluster bootstrap under-covers at strong cluster structure.}
At 76\% empirical coverage, two-way cluster bootstrap is a substantial
improvement over pair-level (17\%) but does not hit nominal 95\%.
With only 12 qualifying targets in the primary macro-avg analysis, we are
firmly in the few-cluster regime where standard cluster bootstrap is known
to under-reject \citep{mackinnon2017wild}.
We additionally compute Rademacher wild cluster bootstrap at the target level
\citep{mackinnon2017wild}: wild CIs for the random-init transformer
are $[-0.079, +0.287]$ versus standard target-bootstrap $[-0.102, +0.281]$,
slightly narrower because Rademacher perturbation preserves variance
structure rather than resampling outlier targets repeatedly.
Directional conclusions are unchanged.
Mixed-effects models with random target intercepts remain a natural
improvement; we report two-way cluster as the minimum-defensible primary
procedure.
The barely-significant lower CI bound for the pairwise contrast should
be interpreted as a diagnostic finding, not a stable superiority claim.

\paragraph{Davis/KIBA pKd transformation.}
We convert Davis raw $K_d$ to $\text{p}K_d$ before z-scoring to align with KIBA
and pChEMBL conventions.
Published DeepDTA results using raw $K_d$ may differ numerically from our
reproduction; we believe our conversion is the correct choice and have
verified that reported trends are consistent with the original DeepDTA
\citep{ozturk2018deepdta}.

\section{Conclusion}

Three evaluation artifacts compound on standard DTI benchmarks: one kinase
dominates the test set, pair-level CIs undercover 5$\times$, and pooled metrics
hide both.
Under proper inference, no model reliably generalises to novel chemistry, and a
randomly-initialised transformer outperforms its pretrained counterpart under
per-target-equal-weight metrics.
The recommendation is simple: macro-averaged per-target Pearson $r$ with
cluster-robust CIs, with pooled $r$ as a flagged secondary metric.
Whether published DTI rankings survive these corrections is an empirical
question the field should be able to answer.
