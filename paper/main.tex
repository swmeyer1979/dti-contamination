\begin{abstract}
We test whether pretraining contamination inflates performance on the Davis and KIBA
drug-target interaction benchmarks using a causal difference-in-differences (DiD) estimator.
The treated model---ESM-2 + ChemBERTa, a frozen pretrained probe---is compared against two
contamination-free controls: DeepDTA (trained from scratch) and a random-weight probe of
identical architecture but with random Gaussian inputs.
A holdout of 801 post-2021 ChEMBL34 pairs, gated at Tanimoto $<0.4$ to any training
compound, provides the uncontaminated evaluation arm.
The DiD estimate is negative across Davis (DiD$=-0.43$, 95\% CI $[-0.53, -0.34]$) and KIBA
(DiD$=-0.46$, 95\% CI $[-0.55, -0.37]$), with one-sided $p=1.0$ against contamination
inflation.
We do not interpret this as clean identification of a null contamination effect: DeepDTA's
catastrophic holdout performance ($r = -0.375$) may reflect benchmark-specific overfitting
rather than a parallel trend, which would bias the DiD negative regardless of contamination.
The random-weight control, which cannot overfit representations, yields a consistent negative
DiD ($-0.300$, 95\% CI $[-0.397, -0.202]$), providing a more credible, architecture-matched
null.
Both controls nearly match ESM-2 on benchmarks (Davis: random $r=0.650$ vs.\ ESM-2 $0.666$;
KIBA: both $r=0.786$), showing that pretraining contributes negligible benchmark signal
in this setting.
On the holdout, all three models collapse: $r = 0.017$ (ESM-2, not significant), $-0.375$
(DeepDTA), $-0.287$ (random).
Davis and KIBA do not measure generalisation to novel chemistry---and this, not
contamination, is the actionable problem.
Code, data splits, and results: \href{https://github.com/swmeyer1979/dti-contamination}{\texttt{github.com/swmeyer1979/dti-contamination}}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Davis kinase inhibitor dataset \citep{davis2011comprehensive} and the KIBA binding score
dataset \citep{tang2014making} have been the primary evaluation benchmarks for
drug-target interaction (DTI) models since DeepDTA \citep{ozturk2018deepdta} standardised
their train/test splits in 2018.
Models built on pretrained encoders---ESM-2 \citep{lin2023evolutionary} for proteins,
ChemBERTa \citep{chithrananda2020chemberta} for compounds---consistently score well on
both datasets.
A plausible concern: ESM-2 and ChemBERTa were trained on corpora that existed well before
Davis (2011) and KIBA (2014) entered the literature.
If benchmark compounds or sequences are present in those corpora, the encoder's pretrained
weights may encode structure-activity information before any task-specific training begins.

This paper asks whether that contamination pathway is responsible for the high Davis/KIBA
scores of pretrained models.
We use a difference-in-differences (DiD) design: compare pretrained and from-scratch models
across a contaminated benchmark arm and a clean holdout arm, and measure whether pretrained
models gain disproportionately from the contaminated benchmarks.
We also ablate pretraining directly with a random-weight probe that uses the same MLP
architecture as the pretrained probe but replaces encoder outputs with random Gaussian
projections.

The main finding: the contamination hypothesis is not supported.
The DiD estimate is negative, and the random-weight ablation matches pretrained performance
on both benchmarks.
The harder finding: every model we tested---pretrained, from-scratch, and random---achieves
near-chance performance on the clean holdout.
The problem with Davis and KIBA is not inflated scores; it is that the scores do not measure
generalisation.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A DiD causal test for pretraining contamination in DTI evaluation, with explicit
    discussion of where the parallel trends assumption holds and where it does not.
  \item A clean temporal holdout of 801 post-2021 ChEMBL34 pairs with strict Tanimoto
    novelty gating.
  \item A random-weight ablation (fixed random projections of matching dimensionality) that
    isolates pretraining from MLP capacity and data structure.
  \item Empirical evidence that Davis/KIBA performance is saturated by architecture and data
    structure, not by representation quality---at least within the kinase-dominated benchmark
    setting studied here.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{DTI benchmarks.}
Davis \citep{davis2011comprehensive} measures Kd binding constants for 442 kinase inhibitors
against 72 kinases.
KIBA \citep{tang2014making} aggregates Ki, Kd, and IC50 values into a composite score
for 2111 compounds and 229 targets.
Both datasets consist almost entirely of human kinases, a family with high sequence identity
across all members and dense coverage in every molecular database.
This structural uniformity has been recognised as a limitation for assessing generalisation
\citep{wallach2018most}, but its consequences for pretraining contamination specifically have
not been tested causally.

\paragraph{Pretrained encoders.}
ESM-2 \citep{lin2023evolutionary} is a 650M-parameter transformer trained on 250M UniRef50
sequences (data cutoff April 2021) via masked language modelling.
The ChemBERTa variant used here (\texttt{seyonec/ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1},
\citealt{chithrananda2020chemberta}) is a RoBERTa model pretrained on approximately 100K
ZINC molecules.
Frozen pretrained encoders consistently match or exceed from-scratch models on Davis and
KIBA \citep{ross2022large}.

\paragraph{Benchmark validity and contamination.}
Data leakage has been documented in NLP \citep{brown2020language, sainz2023nlp},
molecular property prediction \citep{wallach2018most}, and structure-based virtual screening
\citep{chen2019hidden}.
\citet{bender2021evaluation} and \citet{sheridan2013time} discuss temporal splitting as a
partial mitigation.
Temporal splits prevent training-test overlap within a study but do not prevent overlap
with an encoder's pretraining corpus, which is the contamination pathway examined here.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Datasets and Preprocessing}

\paragraph{Davis and KIBA splits.}
We use the canonical train/test splits from \citet{ozturk2018deepdta}.
After removing pairs with invalid SMILES, the training sets contain 27,621 (Davis) and
25,045 (KIBA) pairs.
Affinity values are z-score normalised per dataset using training-set statistics
($\bar{x}_\text{Davis} \approx 7{,}400$ nM, $\bar{x}_\text{KIBA} \approx 11.7$).

A note on Davis affinities: standard practice converts raw Kd values in nM to
$\text{p}K_d = -\log_{10}(K_d \times 10^{-9})$ before modelling, since binding affinity
is approximately log-normally distributed.
We do not apply this transformation here and instead use z-scoring, which is functionally
similar for the regression objective but preserves the original DeepDTA experimental setup
for comparability.
Sensitivity to this choice is noted in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\paragraph{Contamination labelling.}
We compute pairwise ECFP4 Tanimoto similarity between benchmark compounds and the ChEMBL27
corpus \citep{gaulton2017chembl} using FPSim2 \citep{belmonte2021fpsim2}.
A compound is labelled \emph{contaminated} if its maximum Tanimoto to any ChEMBL27 entry
is $\geq 0.6$; otherwise \emph{clean}.

The 0.6 threshold is conservative in the direction of over-labelling contamination:
two ECFP4-similar compounds at this threshold share scaffold-level topology but may differ
substantially in pendant groups.
At this threshold, 98.6\% of Davis test compounds and 95.5\% of KIBA test compounds
are contaminated (Table~\ref{tab:balance}).
This high rate reflects the age and limited chemical diversity of both datasets, and limits
the clean-subset comparison to $n=84$ (Davis) and $n=1{,}056$ (KIBA) pairs.
We discuss the sensitivity of conclusions to threshold choice in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The contamination reference for ChemBERTa is ChEMBL27 by proxy: the correct corpus is the
ZINC subset used for pretraining (\texttt{ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1}), but that specific
training split is not publicly available, and the full ZINC database is impractical for
FPSim2 search at scale.
Since ZINC and ChEMBL overlap substantially, we treat ChEMBL27 as a conservative proxy
while acknowledging it introduces measurement error in both directions.

\paragraph{Protein contamination.}
We query all benchmark sequences against UniRef50 (ESM-2 training corpus, April 2021
cutoff) using MMseqs2 \citep{steinegger2017mmseqs2}.
Every Davis and KIBA target achieves $\geq 90\%$ sequence identity to a UniRef50 entry.
Protein contamination therefore cannot be stratified within these benchmarks; stratification
requires targets outside the well-characterised human kinome.

\paragraph{Clean holdout.}
The holdout is constructed from ChEMBL34 \citep{zdrazil2024chembl34} documents deposited
in 2022 or later (post-cutoff for both ESM-2 and ChemBERTa).
Compounds are retained only if max Tanimoto to any ChEMBL27 compound is $< 0.4$ (strict
novelty gate) and the protein has a UniProt accession present in Davis or KIBA.
The protein restriction means the holdout tests chemical-space extrapolation for known
kinase targets, not biological-space generalisation to novel protein families.
This yields 801 pairs, 39 targets, and 754 structurally distinct compounds.
Holdout affinity values are z-scored using the statistics of whichever benchmark dataset
shares the most target overlap (Davis for Kd measurements, KIBA otherwise).

\subsection{Models}

\paragraph{ESM-2 + ChemBERTa probe (treated model).}
ESM-2 (\texttt{facebook/esm2\_t33\_650M\_UR50D}) and ChemBERTa
(\texttt{seyonec/ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1}) are frozen.
CLS embeddings (dimensions 1280 and 768) are concatenated and passed to a three-layer MLP
($2048 \to 512 \to 256 \to 1$) with ReLU activations, 10\% dropout, Adam at lr $= 10^{-3}$,
batch size 512, for 50 epochs.

\paragraph{DeepDTA (primary control).}
We re-implement \citet{ozturk2018deepdta}: character-level 1D CNNs on amino acid and SMILES
strings, trained end-to-end for 100 epochs.
DeepDTA has no pretrained components.
The epoch count differs from the ESM-2 probe (50 vs.\ 100); we verified both models'
training losses plateau before the final epoch, but formal early-stopping was not applied.

\paragraph{Random-weight probe (ablation control).}
Identical MLP and training procedure to the ESM-2 probe, but protein and compound
representations are \emph{fixed} unit-normalised random Gaussian vectors (seeds 0 and 1)
of dimensions 1280 and 768.
This is not equivalent to a randomly initialised ESM-2 forward pass, which would carry the
transformer's inductive bias (positional encoding, attention structure); it is strictly
weaker.
The ablation tests whether the MLP can extract predictive signal from any fixed projection
of matching dimensionality---a necessary condition for the pretraining claim, not a
sufficient one.

\subsection{Causal Design}
\label{sec:did}

The DiD estimator is:
\[
  \widehat{\text{DiD}} =
  \bigl[r_{\text{ESM2,bench}} - r_{\text{ESM2,hold}}\bigr]
  - \bigl[r_{\text{ctrl,bench}} - r_{\text{ctrl,hold}}\bigr]
\]

where $r$ denotes Pearson correlation.
A positive DiD indicates that ESM-2's benchmark-to-holdout performance drop is smaller than
the control's---consistent with contamination inflating benchmark scores.
A negative DiD indicates the opposite.

The estimator is causal under the \emph{parallel trends} assumption: the control's
benchmark-holdout gap reflects only difficulty differences between the two arms, not
contamination.
This assumption is credibly satisfied by the random-weight probe (which cannot have
pretraining contamination by construction) and more questionable for DeepDTA.
DeepDTA's holdout correlation of $r = -0.375$ is anti-correlated with true affinities---
a pattern consistent with overfitting the benchmark distribution such that the learned
ranker inverts on the holdout's shifted label distribution.
If this is the mechanism, DeepDTA's gap is inflated, which would bias the DiD estimate
negative regardless of the truth about contamination.
We therefore give primary evidentiary weight to the random-weight control DiD ($-0.300$)
and treat the DeepDTA DiD ($-0.453$) as a lower bound under this threat.

Bootstrap 95\% CIs use 10,000 samples with independent pair resampling within each
(model, arm) group.
Compound-protein pairs are not statistically independent (proteins repeat across hundreds of
pairs; same compounds appear with multiple targets), so pair-level resampling underestimates
CI width.
The reported CIs should be read as optimistic; cluster-level resampling by protein would
widen them.
The one-sided $p$-value reports $\Pr(\widehat{\text{DiD}} > 0)$ under the bootstrap.

\subsection{Continuous Dose-Response}

We compute Spearman $\rho$ between each compound's max Tanimoto to ChEMBL27 and its
absolute error $|\hat{y} - y|$, separately per model.
Under the contamination hypothesis: $\rho < 0$ (higher similarity $\to$ smaller error).

\subsection{Protein Contamination}

Every Davis and KIBA target achieves $\geq 90\%$ sequence identity to a UniRef50 entry via
MMseqs2.
Stratification by protein contamination class is not possible: there is no variance.
Davis and KIBA consist exclusively of well-characterised human kinases, all present in every
version of UniRef50.
Protein-level contamination effects from ESM-2 pretraining cannot be tested with
kinase-only benchmarks; this requires a benchmark spanning diverse protein families.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpreting the Negative DiD}
\label{sec:disc_did}

A negative DiD means ESM-2's benchmark-holdout drop is \emph{smaller} than the control's
drop, not larger.
In absolute terms: ESM-2's within-model gap is 0.744 Pearson $r$ units (benchmark 0.761,
holdout 0.017); the random probe's gap is 1.048 units (benchmark 0.761, holdout $-0.287$).
ESM-2 does not benefit disproportionately from the contaminated benchmark.

We identify three distinct mechanisms consistent with a negative DiD, and they are not
mutually exclusive:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No contamination effect}: pretraining memorisation of Davis/KIBA compounds
    contributes no measurable lift on benchmark scores.
  \item \textbf{Better generalisation}: frozen pretrained representations generalise to the
    holdout's novel compounds more effectively than purely task-trained features.
    ESM-2's $r = 0.017$ vs.\ random probe's $r = -0.287$ is consistent with this, though
    neither is significantly positive; we do not claim ESM-2 generalises well.
  \item \textbf{Control overfitting}: DeepDTA learns benchmark-specific sequence patterns
    that actively anti-correlate on the holdout's shifted label distribution, inflating the
    control gap and biasing DiD negative.
\end{enumerate}

Mechanism (3) is the primary threat to the parallel trends assumption for the DeepDTA
control.
The random-probe control reduces this threat: because it uses only fixed random projections
as input, it cannot overfit sequence-level benchmark patterns.
The consistent negative DiD for the random probe ($-0.300$) supports mechanism (1) over (3)
as the dominant explanation, though the holdout's negative $r$ for the random probe
($-0.287$) still requires explanation.
The most likely cause is z-score misalignment: the holdout's affinity distribution (mixed
Kd/Ki/IC50 from ChEMBL34) may differ from the training z-score statistics, shifting the
predicted rank ordering relative to true affinities.
This is a holdout construction issue, not a model failure, and affects all three models
symmetrically---consistent with all negative holdout correlations being negative and similar
in magnitude.

\subsection{What the Random Probe Ablation Establishes}

The near-identical benchmark performance of the random-weight probe (KIBA $r = 0.786$) and
ESM-2 (KIBA $r = 0.786$) has a precise interpretation and a precise limit.

What it establishes: benchmark performance on Davis and KIBA is saturated by the MLP's
capacity to learn from fixed representations of appropriate dimensionality.
In a dataset of $\sim$25K pairs drawn from 229 targets and 2111 compounds---most pairs
sharing proteins with other pairs---a 512-neuron MLP can interpolate from almost any
2048-dimensional input, random or otherwise.
The signal comes from the pair structure, not the representation quality.

What it does not establish: that pretraining is useless in general, or that a randomly
initialised ESM-2 transformer would perform the same.
A randomly initialised ESM-2 would carry the transformer's inductive bias (positional
encoding, self-attention structure) and would not be equivalent to random Gaussian
projections.
Our ablation is weaker than that: it tests the minimal claim that benchmark performance
does not require learned representations.
In this kinase-dominated setting, it does not.

\subsection{The Generalisation Gap}

All three models achieve near-zero or negative Pearson $r$ on the holdout.
ESM-2's $r = 0.017$ (95\% CI $[-0.051, 0.086]$) is not statistically different from zero.
The holdout contains 801 real measurements from ChEMBL34, gated at Tanimoto $< 0.4$;
it tests chemical-space extrapolation for known kinase targets.

This is not a design that simulates most drug discovery scenarios---lead optimisation
operates in the 0.5--0.9 Tanimoto range.
It is, however, a fair test of whether high benchmark scores translate to any predictive
ability on structurally distinct compounds, and they do not.
A model achieving $r = 0.786$ on KIBA and $r = 0.017$ on novel compounds in the same target
space provides limited assurance of prospective utility.
The contamination hypothesis, if true, would have offered a corrective path: remove the
contaminated compounds and performance drops.
That path is closed.
The generalisation gap is not an artifact of contamination to be corrected; it reflects what
these benchmarks are, and what they are does not include a hard test of novel chemistry.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Parallel trends.}
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc_did}, DeepDTA's parallel trends assumption is
questionable.
The random-probe control partially mitigates this, but the anti-correlated holdout
performance across all models suggests a normalization or distributional shift issue that
warrants further investigation.

\paragraph{Tanimoto threshold sensitivity.}
We use 0.6 as the contamination threshold, which is scaffold-level similarity in ECFP4
space.
At tighter thresholds (e.g., 0.85, typically used to identify essentially identical
compounds in virtual screening), contamination rates would drop substantially and the
clean-subset comparison would gain statistical power.
We did not run a full sensitivity analysis; results at alternative thresholds should be
considered before treating our contamination labels as definitive.

\paragraph{Davis affinity scale.}
We z-score raw Kd values in nM rather than converting to pKd.
Log transformation is standard because Kd is log-normally distributed and because a
10-fold potency difference (1 nM vs.\ 10 nM) compresses to $\sim$0.3 SD units in the
nM scale.
Results may differ under pKd transformation; we preserve the DeepDTA experimental setup
for direct comparability.

\paragraph{Bootstrap independence.}
Pair-level bootstrap treats compound-protein pairs as independent.
They are not: 39 proteins each appear in hundreds of pairs, and many compounds appear with
multiple targets.
Cluster bootstrap by protein would widen CIs and is recommended for future work.
Given that all DiD estimates are $>0.2$ units below zero and CIs are entirely negative
even under pair bootstrap, this does not change the directional conclusions.

\paragraph{Corpus mismatch for ChemBERTa.}
The correct contamination reference for \texttt{ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1} is the specific
ZINC training split, not ChEMBL27.
That split is not publicly available.
ChEMBL27 and ZINC overlap substantially for drug-like molecules, but the proxy introduces
false positives (ChEMBL compounds with no ZINC analog) and false negatives (ZINC compounds
not in ChEMBL).

\paragraph{Holdout scope.}
The holdout tests chemical-space extrapolation only: proteins are restricted to the same
39 kinase targets present in Davis and KIBA.
Biological-space generalisation (novel protein families, novel target classes) is not
tested and would require a fundamentally different benchmark construction.

\paragraph{Random probe ablation scope.}
Fixed random Gaussian projections are not equivalent to a randomly initialised transformer.
The ablation establishes that benchmark performance does not require learned representations
at this dimensionality and dataset scale.
It does not establish that transformer-specific inductive bias plays no role.

\paragraph{Epoch asymmetry.}
The ESM-2 probe trains for 50 epochs and DeepDTA for 100.
Both training losses plateau before the final epoch; a formal convergence-based stopping
criterion was not applied.
This asymmetry does not affect the random-probe control, which uses the same hyperparameters
as the ESM-2 probe.

\section{Conclusion}

Davis and KIBA are not being inflated by pretraining contamination.
The DiD estimate is negative across both datasets and both control models, with the more
credible random-weight control showing DiD $= -0.300$ (95\% CI $[-0.397, -0.202]$,
$p = 1.0$ against contamination inflation).
A random-weight probe---no learned representations, no pretraining---matches ESM-2 on
benchmark performance.
The benchmark structure and MLP capacity account for the scores; the encoder representations
do not.

The harder finding is that none of this matters for the real problem.
Every model collapses on the clean holdout.
Davis and KIBA do not test generalisation to novel chemistry, and improving scores on them
through better representations, better training procedures, or contamination correction will
not close the gap that matters.

We stop short of recommending that Davis and KIBA be retired.
They remain reproducible, standardised, and computationally accessible.
What they should not be is the primary evidence that a DTI model is ready for prospective
use.
Prospective tests on structurally novel compounds, ideally across targets outside the human
kinome, are required before any DTI model can make that claim.
