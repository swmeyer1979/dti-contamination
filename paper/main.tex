\begin{abstract}
We test whether pretraining corpus contamination inflates performance on the Davis
and KIBA drug-target interaction benchmarks using a difference-in-differences (DiD)
estimator.
A frozen ESM-2 + ChemBERTa probe (treated) is compared against three
contamination-free controls---DeepDTA (trained from scratch), a random-weight probe
with fixed Gaussian projections, and a randomly-initialised ESM-2/ChemBERTa
transformer of identical architecture---on Davis, KIBA, and a 801-pair clean
holdout of post-2021 ChEMBL34 activities gated at Tanimoto $<0.4$ to training
compounds.
The point estimates of DiD are small and inconsistent in sign: $-0.24$ against DeepDTA,
$+0.01$ against the fixed-projection random probe, and $+0.06$ against the random
transformer.
Under protein-cluster bootstrap---the correct inference procedure when pairs share
proteins---every 95\% CI crosses zero ($p$ range 0.37--0.77).
Under pair-level bootstrap, which the literature commonly reports but which
assumes independent pairs, the DeepDTA comparison is significant and others are
not; this discrepancy is a statistical artifact rather than a finding.
We therefore cannot reject no-contamination, cannot confirm it, and report a more
informative null than past work has.
Two substantive findings survive proper inference: pretraining matters---a
randomly-initialised transformer achieves only $r\approx0.52$ on Davis and KIBA
versus $0.78$--$0.80$ for the pretrained probe---but on novel chemistry the same
pretrained probe reaches only $r = 0.132$ on the clean holdout.
Davis and KIBA score saturation is driven by pair structure and pretraining in roughly
equal measure; neither translates to predictive power on compounds outside the
training manifold.
Code, data splits, and results: \href{https://github.com/swmeyer1979/dti-contamination}{\texttt{github.com/swmeyer1979/dti-contamination}}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Davis kinase inhibitor dataset \citep{davis2011comprehensive} and the KIBA binding
score dataset \citep{tang2014making} have been the primary evaluation benchmarks for
drug-target interaction (DTI) models since DeepDTA \citep{ozturk2018deepdta}
standardised their train/test splits in 2018.
Models built on pretrained encoders---ESM-2 \citep{lin2023evolutionary} for proteins,
ChemBERTa \citep{chithrananda2020chemberta} for compounds---consistently score well on
both datasets.
A plausible concern: ESM-2 and ChemBERTa were trained on corpora that existed well
before Davis (2011) and KIBA (2014) entered the literature.
If benchmark compounds or sequences appear in those corpora, the encoder weights may
encode structure-activity information before any task-specific training begins.

This paper asks whether that contamination pathway is identifiable, and if so,
whether it meaningfully inflates Davis/KIBA scores.
We use a difference-in-differences design: compare pretrained and contamination-free
models across a contaminated benchmark arm and a clean holdout arm, with the DiD
measuring whether the pretrained model gains disproportionately from the benchmark.
To isolate pretraining from architecture, we include two architecture-matched controls:
a random-weight probe that uses fixed Gaussian projections in place of encoder
outputs, and a randomly-initialised transformer of identical architecture to ESM-2
and ChemBERTa but without pretrained weights.

The central finding is a negative one: under protein-cluster bootstrap, which
accounts for the non-independence of (protein, compound) pairs that share proteins,
none of the DiD estimates are statistically distinguishable from zero.
Pair-level bootstrap---the standard inference procedure for these benchmarks---
produces narrower CIs that label the DeepDTA comparison significant, but this
appears to be an artifact of treating 229 proteins times 100 compounds as though
they were 23,000 independent observations.
We therefore make no claim about whether contamination inflates these benchmarks.
We do report two substantive findings that the analysis does identify: pretraining
contributes materially to benchmark performance (a random transformer loses $\sim$0.3
in Pearson $r$ versus the pretrained probe), and none of the models generalises well
to novel chemistry (ESM-2 achieves $r = 0.132$ on the holdout).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A DiD analysis of contamination in Davis/KIBA with three architecturally
    distinct controls, evaluated under both pair-level and protein-cluster bootstrap.
  \item An argument that cluster bootstrap is the correct inference procedure for
    these benchmarks, and a demonstration that it changes the verdict.
  \item Evidence that pretrained weights (not just transformer inductive bias)
    materially contribute to benchmark scores, from a randomly-initialised ESM-2
    ablation.
  \item A clean holdout of 801 post-2021 ChEMBL34 pairs with strict Tanimoto
    novelty gating, showing that benchmark performance on kinase-family targets
    does not predict performance on structurally novel compounds.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{DTI benchmarks.}
Davis \citep{davis2011comprehensive} measures $K_d$ binding constants for 442 kinase
inhibitors against 72 kinases.
KIBA \citep{tang2014making} aggregates $K_i$, $K_d$, and IC\textsubscript{50} values
into a composite score for 2,111 compounds and 229 targets.
Both datasets consist almost entirely of human kinases, a family with high sequence
identity across members and dense coverage in every molecular database.
This structural uniformity has been recognised as a limitation for assessing
generalisation \citep{wallach2018most}, but its consequences for pretraining
contamination have not previously been tested causally.

\paragraph{Pretrained encoders.}
ESM-2 \citep{lin2023evolutionary} is a 650M-parameter transformer trained on 250M
UniRef50 sequences (data cutoff April 2021) via masked language modelling.
The ChemBERTa variant used here (\texttt{seyonec/ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1},
\citealt{chithrananda2020chemberta}) is a RoBERTa model pretrained on approximately
100K ZINC molecules.
Frozen pretrained encoders consistently match or exceed from-scratch models on
Davis and KIBA \citep{ross2022large}.

\paragraph{Benchmark validity and contamination.}
Data leakage has been documented in NLP \citep{brown2020language, sainz2023nlp},
molecular property prediction \citep{wallach2018most}, and structure-based virtual
screening \citep{chen2019hidden}.
\citet{bender2021evaluation} and \citet{sheridan2013time} discuss temporal
splitting as a partial mitigation, but temporal splits do not address overlap
between benchmark compounds and an encoder's pretraining corpus, which is the
pathway examined here.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Datasets and Preprocessing}

\paragraph{Davis and KIBA splits.}
We use the canonical train/test splits from \citet{ozturk2018deepdta}.
After removing pairs with invalid SMILES, the training sets contain 27,621 (Davis)
and 25,045 (KIBA) pairs.

Davis $K_d$ values are stored in the canonical DeepDTA release as raw nM
concentrations (mean 7{,}416 nM, range 0.02--10{,}000 nM).
We convert to $\text{p}K_d = -\log_{10}(K_d \cdot 10^{-9})$ before normalisation
so that higher values denote stronger binding.
This is essential: without the transformation, Davis uses the opposite
sign convention to the clean holdout's pChEMBL values, and models trained jointly
on raw $K_d$ Davis + KIBA scores will anti-correlate with pChEMBL labels at
inference time, producing spurious negative correlations on the holdout.
After transformation, Davis has mean $\text{p}K_d = 5.45$ and standard deviation
$0.89$; all three arms (Davis, KIBA, holdout) share the convention that higher
values indicate stronger binding.

Affinity values are then z-score normalised per dataset using training-set
statistics before model training.
Pearson $r$ is invariant to this transformation.
The clean holdout has no training split, so we z-score it using its own test
statistics (mean $\text{pChEMBL} = 8.65$, s.d. $1.56$); the resulting predictions
and labels share zero mean and unit variance within the holdout, matching the
convention used for Davis and KIBA.

\paragraph{Compound contamination labelling.}
We compute pairwise ECFP4 Tanimoto similarity between benchmark compounds and the
ChEMBL27 corpus \citep{gaulton2017chembl} using FPSim2 \citep{belmonte2021fpsim2}.
A compound is labelled \emph{contaminated} at threshold $\tau$ if its maximum
Tanimoto to any ChEMBL27 entry is $\geq \tau$; otherwise \emph{clean}.
Primary analysis uses $\tau = 0.6$ (scaffold-level similarity).
We report sensitivity at $\tau \in \{0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.85\}$ in
Section~\ref{sec:threshold}.

The contamination reference for ChemBERTa is ChEMBL27 by proxy: the exact
ZINC training split used to pretrain \texttt{ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1} is not
publicly available, and the full ZINC database is impractical for FPSim2 search at
scale.
ZINC and ChEMBL overlap substantially for drug-like molecules; we treat ChEMBL27 as
a conservative proxy with measurement error in both directions.

\paragraph{Protein contamination.}
We query all benchmark sequences against UniRef50 (ESM-2 training corpus, April 2021
cutoff) using MMseqs2 \citep{steinegger2017mmseqs2}.
Every Davis and KIBA target achieves $\geq 90\%$ sequence identity to a UniRef50
entry.
Protein contamination therefore has zero variance in these benchmarks and is not
stratifiable; we report this as a null finding and move on.

\paragraph{Clean holdout.}
The holdout is constructed from ChEMBL34 \citep{zdrazil2024chembl34} documents
deposited in 2022 or later (post-cutoff for both ESM-2 and ChemBERTa).
Compounds are retained only if max Tanimoto to any ChEMBL27 compound is $<0.4$
(strict novelty gate) and the protein has a UniProt accession present in Davis or
KIBA.
The protein restriction means the holdout tests chemical-space extrapolation for
known kinase targets, not biological-space generalisation.
This yields 801 pairs, 39 targets, and 754 structurally distinct compounds.

\subsection{Models}

\paragraph{ESM-2 + ChemBERTa probe (treated model).}
ESM-2 (\texttt{facebook/esm2\_t33\_650M\_UR50D}) and ChemBERTa
(\texttt{seyonec/ChemBERTa-zinc-base-v1}) are frozen.
CLS embeddings (dimensions 1280 and 768) are concatenated and passed to a three-layer
MLP ($2048 \to 512 \to 256 \to 1$) with ReLU activations, 10\% dropout, Adam at lr
$=10^{-3}$, batch size 512, for 50 epochs.

\paragraph{DeepDTA (primary from-scratch control).}
We re-implement \citet{ozturk2018deepdta}: character-level 1D CNNs on amino acid and
SMILES strings, trained end-to-end for 100 epochs.
DeepDTA has no pretrained components.
Both models' training losses plateau well before the final epoch.

\paragraph{Random-weight probe (fixed-projection control).}
Identical MLP and training procedure to the ESM-2 probe, but protein and compound
representations are \emph{fixed} unit-normalised Gaussian vectors (seeds 0 and 1)
of dimensions 1280 and 768.
This tests whether the MLP can extract predictive signal from any fixed projection
of appropriate dimensionality---a weaker ablation than the random transformer
below, but useful for isolating MLP capacity from representation content.

\paragraph{Random-transformer probe (randomly-initialised architecture control).}
The key ablation: same ESM-2 and ChemBERTa architectures---identical configs loaded
via \texttt{EsmConfig.from\_pretrained} and \texttt{RobertaConfig.from\_pretrained}---
but with randomly-initialised weights rather than pretrained weights.
CLS embeddings are row-normalised before concatenation (otherwise random weights
produce unstable embedding magnitudes).
The MLP head is identical to the ESM-2 probe.
This ablation tests whether transformer architectural inductive bias (attention,
positional encoding) alone explains benchmark performance, or whether learned
pretrained weights contribute materially.

\subsection{Causal Design and Inference}
\label{sec:did}

The DiD estimator is:
\[
  \widehat{\text{DiD}} =
  \bigl[r_{\text{ESM2,bench}} - r_{\text{ESM2,hold}}\bigr]
  - \bigl[r_{\text{ctrl,bench}} - r_{\text{ctrl,hold}}\bigr]
\]
where $r$ denotes Pearson correlation.
Positive DiD: ESM-2's benchmark-to-holdout drop is smaller than the control's,
consistent with contamination inflating benchmark scores.
Negative DiD: the opposite.

The estimator is causal under the \emph{parallel trends} assumption: the control's
benchmark-holdout gap reflects only difficulty differences, not contamination.
This is credible for the two random controls (which cannot have pretraining
contamination) and weaker for DeepDTA (which can overfit benchmark-specific
sequence patterns).
We report DiD against all three controls and read the null against all three.

\paragraph{Bootstrap inference.}
We report two bootstrap procedures (10{,}000 resamples each, seed 42):

\emph{Pair-level bootstrap.}
Each $(y_\text{true}, y_\text{pred})$ pair is treated as an independent observation;
the full test set is resampled with replacement.
This is the standard procedure in the DTI literature.

\emph{Protein-cluster bootstrap.}
Proteins are resampled with replacement; all pairs for each sampled protein are
retained together.
Davis has 72 unique targets; KIBA has 229.
Each protein contributes 80--100 pairs.
Because all Pearson $r$ values in our benchmark are computed across pairs that share
a small set of proteins, pair-level bootstrap underestimates CI width by assuming
independence that does not exist.
Cluster bootstrap correctly propagates the within-protein correlation of residuals.
We treat cluster bootstrap as the primary inference procedure.

\paragraph{One-sided $p$-value.}
We report $p = \Pr(\widehat{\text{DiD}}^* \leq 0)$ over the bootstrap distribution,
where $\widehat{\text{DiD}}^* \leq 0$ is the event of interest against the
contamination hypothesis (DiD $> 0$).
$p$ close to 1 therefore indicates strong evidence \emph{against} contamination
inflation; $p$ close to 0 indicates evidence \emph{for} it; intermediate values
reflect uncertainty.

\subsection{Continuous Dose-Response}

Spearman $\rho$ between compound max Tanimoto to ChEMBL27 and absolute prediction
error is near zero for all four models: $|\rho| < 0.02$ and, where significant,
inconsistent in sign across models.
This analysis mirrors the threshold sensitivity conclusion: there is no
dose-response in the direction contamination would predict.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the DiD Analysis Does and Does Not Establish}

The central DiD result is that we cannot distinguish contamination from
no-contamination under cluster bootstrap, and we can under pair bootstrap only
because pair bootstrap is wrong for this data structure.
Davis has 72 proteins each appearing in $\sim$85 pairs; KIBA has 229 proteins
each appearing in $\sim$103 pairs; the holdout has 39 proteins each appearing in
$\sim$20 pairs.
Pair-level resampling treats these as independent and obtains CIs on the order of
$\pm$0.10 around a DiD point estimate of $-0.25$.
Cluster bootstrap correctly recognises the small number of distinct proteins and
returns CIs on the order of $\pm$0.55.
The apparent precision of the pair-level estimate is mostly artefactual.

This does not mean contamination has no effect.
It means this design, with these controls on these benchmarks, cannot identify
such an effect if one exists within $\pm$0.5 Pearson $r$ of zero.
A DiD analysis capable of detecting a smaller effect would need either many more
distinct proteins (breaking the human-kinome homogeneity of Davis/KIBA), better
instrumental variation (e.g., benchmarks stratified by encoder cutoff date), or
far larger clean-holdout $n$.

\subsection{Pretraining Contributes Real Signal; Contamination May or May Not}

The random-transformer result is the strongest positive finding in this paper.
A transformer with identical architecture to the pretrained ESM-2/ChemBERTa but
with random weights achieves $r \approx 0.52$ on both benchmarks versus $r \approx
0.79$ for the pretrained version.
A gap of $\Delta r \approx 0.27$ from pretraining alone is not small.
This falsifies the strongest form of our prior work's framing, which suggested that
benchmark performance is saturated by architecture and data structure and that
pretraining contributes negligibly.
It does not.

What the random-transformer result does not do is tell us whether the pretraining
benefit comes from memorisation of benchmark compounds specifically, from learning
generally useful representations of kinase-binding chemistry, or from some
combination.
The DiD analysis was intended to answer that question and does not answer it
conclusively.

\subsection{The Holdout Is the Binding Constraint}

ESM-2 + ChemBERTa achieves $r = 0.132$ on the clean holdout: a real but modest
positive correlation.
DeepDTA achieves $r = -0.042$, statistically indistinguishable from zero.
For context, the fixed-projection random probe achieves $r = 0.152$, which
outperforms ESM-2; this is consistent with the random probe's predictions
effectively encoding per-protein means (the fixed Gaussian vector for each
kinase is a stable identifier), and the holdout reusing those 39 kinases.

The holdout is harder than the benchmarks in a way that penalises all learnt
structure except per-target mean affinity.
A model that achieves $r = 0.85$ on KIBA and $r = 0.15$ on novel compounds for
the same 39 targets does not have an identification problem with contamination;
it has a representation problem.
Contamination, if present, is a minor correction to a scoring rule that does not
predict prospective activity.

\subsection{Where Does This Leave Davis and KIBA?}

Davis and KIBA are not being invalidated by this analysis.
They remain reproducible, standardised, and useful for comparative model
development.
What they cannot support is a claim that high scores indicate prospective
utility on novel chemistry, even within the same target family.
Any such claim requires a clean-holdout evaluation with sufficient novel $n$ to
support a meaningful correlation, and a bootstrap procedure that recognises
the correlated-pair structure of DTI test sets.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Limited power to detect small contamination effects.}
Cluster bootstrap CIs span $\pm$0.55 around the DiD point estimates.
An effect of $0.05$--$0.10$---the magnitude of the clean-contaminated gap
at $\tau = 0.6$---is not distinguishable from zero under this procedure.
We cannot rule out contamination at that magnitude.

\paragraph{Parallel trends for DeepDTA.}
DeepDTA achieves $r = -0.042$ on the holdout, which is not anti-correlated
(unlike in an earlier version of this analysis before the $K_d \to \text{p}K_d$
transformation was applied), but the benchmark-holdout gap is still the largest
among all controls.
If DeepDTA is overfitting benchmark-specific patterns beyond what a contamination-free
control should, the DeepDTA DiD is biased negative.
The two random controls, which cannot overfit sequence patterns, produce DiD near
zero; this is consistent with but does not prove that DeepDTA's negative DiD
reflects control misspecification rather than absence of contamination.

\paragraph{Random-transformer limitations.}
Randomly-initialised ESM-2 produces embedding magnitudes that differ substantially
from pretrained ESM-2; we row-normalise to mitigate but not eliminate this.
The random transformer's weak benchmark performance ($r \approx 0.52$) is
consistent with the transformer's inductive bias being useful but insufficient
without training.
We do not claim the gap between random and pretrained is a measurement of
pretraining's causal contribution to \emph{contamination}; it is a measurement
of pretraining's causal contribution to benchmark performance overall.

\paragraph{Contamination reference is a proxy.}
The ChemBERTa variant used was pretrained on a ZINC subset, not ChEMBL27.
The exact pretraining split is not public and the full ZINC database is
impractical for FPSim2 search.
ChEMBL27 overlaps substantially with ZINC but introduces measurement error
(false positives and false negatives) in contamination labels.

\paragraph{Threshold sensitivity does not resolve threshold choice.}
Results are qualitatively similar across $\tau \in [0.5, 0.85]$, but the absolute
size of the contaminated-clean gap varies by threshold.
The null conclusion holds at every threshold; any positive conclusion at a
specific threshold would need to explain why that threshold is privileged.

\paragraph{Protein contamination is unstratifiable.}
Davis/KIBA are $\geq 90\%$ UniRef50 identity for every target.
Any protein-contamination effect is absorbed into the (untestable) base rate;
this paper cannot speak to protein-level contamination at all.

\paragraph{Holdout scope.}
The holdout restricts to the 39 kinase targets present in Davis/KIBA.
Generalisation to novel protein families is a different question and would
require a different benchmark.

\paragraph{Bootstrap seed dependence.}
Cluster CIs with 10{,}000 resamples have residual Monte Carlo variation on the
order of $\pm$0.01 in each endpoint.
Conclusions are robust to this, but individual CI endpoints should not be
over-interpreted to three decimal places.

\section{Conclusion}

Under proper inference, the DiD analysis is a double null: we cannot conclude
contamination inflates Davis and KIBA, and we cannot conclude it does not.
The design identifies an effect that would have to be large ($|\text{DiD}| > 0.5$)
for this data structure to detect, and no effect of that size is present.
Whether a smaller effect exists is beyond what these benchmarks can answer.

Three findings do survive proper inference:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pretraining contributes materially to benchmark performance: a randomly-initialised transformer drops $r$ by $\sim$0.27 relative to pretrained on both benchmarks.
  \item Davis and KIBA performance does not translate to novel chemistry: the best holdout Pearson $r$ across all models is $0.152$.
  \item Pair-level bootstrap CIs on DTI benchmarks are systematically too narrow. The literature convention of reporting them without acknowledging the protein-clustered structure of the data inflates apparent significance.
\end{itemize}

The actionable recommendation is narrow and specific: DTI papers comparing
pretrained and from-scratch models should report cluster-bootstrap CIs by default.
Whether this changes published conclusions in any specific case is an empirical
question the community should be able to answer.
